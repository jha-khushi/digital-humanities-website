Khushi Jha’s Digital Humanities Portfolio

Portfolio introduction. These four assignments ask what computational methods can and cannot do with Indian languages and literature. They move from preparing Hindi text for NLP, to retrieving Kalidasa’s plants and animals with a local RAG pipeline, to a synthetic reading of Tagore’s “Subha” that tests a literary argument with an LLM and visualisation, and finally to writing a SKILL that makes an LLM brief Indian constitutional judgments faithfully. Each exhibition below links to its files on GitHub.

Assignment 01

NLP: Indic Stop Words — Hindi

Abstract. Stop words are high-frequency function words that carry little content on their own and are usually removed before keyword extraction, topic modelling or frequency analysis. This file compiles a Hindi (Devanagari script) stop-word list grouped by grammatical category — conjunctions, pronouns, possessives, postpositions, question words, auxiliary verbs and particles — with a small function that removes them from a sentence.

File: hindi_stopwords.py

Visible assignment work

Sample from the list (235 words in total):

और · या · लेकिन · क्योंकि · मैं · हम · वह · यह · मेरा · उनका · अपना · का · की · के · में · से · ने · को · क्या · कौन · है · था · नहीं · भी

Example. Removing stop words from “मैं आज अपने दोस्त के साथ बाज़ार गई और वहाँ से किताबें खरीदीं” (“Today I went to the market with my friend and bought books there”) leaves only the content words “दोस्त बाज़ार किताबें खरीदीं” (friend, market, books, bought).

Limitations. Hindi spelling varies (गए / गये, नहीं / नही), so common variants are listed together. Negation words such as नहीं change meaning, so they should be kept for tasks like sentiment analysis.

Assignment 02

Extraction of Indian Cultural Elements Using RAG: Flora and Fauna in Kalidasa

Abstract. Kalidasa’s poetry and drama are famous for their dense natural world: hermitage groves, seasonal flowers and the birds of Sanskrit convention. Using a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline that runs entirely on a local machine, I built a repository of Kalidasa’s seven works in Arthur W. Ryder’s public-domain translation, retrieved the passages most relevant to plants and animals, and had a local LLM extract their names. Every name was checked against its source passage and validated, giving 28 plants and 29 animals.

Result file: flora_fauna.py

Pipeline: corpus (Kalidasa, tr. Ryder) · preprocess.py · rag_pipeline.py · validate_results.py · make_flora_fauna.py · validated.json (each name with its evidence sentence)

Visible assignment work

Pipeline. (1) Pre-process: remove Project Gutenberg licence text and split the book into its seven works, leaving out the translator’s introduction. (2) Chunk into 300 overlapping passages of about 250 words. (3) Embed each passage with nomic-embed-text. (4) Retrieve the 15 most similar passages for each of 12 queries (trees, flowers, creepers, crops, mammals, birds, reptiles, fish, insects…), 90 passages in all. (5) Generate: qwen2.5:7b lists the plant and animal names in each passage as JSON. (6) Verify that each name occurs in its passage, then validate it (the model, plus recorded author corrections) and merge variants. (7) Save as flora_fauna.py.

Flora (28): trees: ashoka, bakul, mango, deodar, coral tree, fig, jujube, almond, bamboo, nipa; flowers: lotus, water-lily, lily, jasmine, siris, ketak (screw-pine), lodh, amaranth, trumpet-flower; grasses and herbs: sacred grass (kusha), panic grass, reed, madder, mushroom; crops and fruit: rice, plantain, rose-apple; creeper: spring creeper.

Fauna (29): mammals: elephant, lion, tiger, deer, antelope, bear, boar, bison, buffalo, yak, cow, jackal, ape; birds: peacock, cuckoo, swan, ruddy goose (chakravaka), crane, heron, parrot, plover, starling, nightingale; reptiles: cobra, snake; others: bee, spider, carp, frog.

What the validation step showed. Of 165 raw names from the LLM, only 101 were real plants or animals. The rest were ornaments (“anklets”, “lotus-bracelet”), places (“Ganges river”, “hermitage”), a character (“Shakuntala”) or the moon, and many were variants of one name (“mango-blossoms”, “mango-twig”). This is itself a finding: in Kalidasa, plants and animals are so bound up with ornament, ritual and the heroine’s body (lotus-bracelets, bark dresses, the “vine-bride”) that a model reading for “nature” cannot easily separate the natural world from the cultural one.

Limitations. Names come from an English translation (1912), so some are the translator’s choices (“nightingale” and “starling” for Indian birds); retrieval finds only the most relevant passages, not every mention.

Assignment 03

Synthetic Reading: Voice, Silence and Nature in Tagore’s “Subha”

Abstract. “Subha” is the story of a mute village girl whose family treats her as a “silent weight” and finally marries her off without revealing her muteness. After a partial reading of 20% of the story, I asked how the text makes speech the condition for being recognised as a feeling person, and where Subha’s own language goes when no human hears it. A local LLM extracted expressions along four dimensions (voice and silence, nature and non-human kinship, social and patriarchal structure, affect) across three stages of the story. The visualisation shows that Subha’s language lives with the river and the cows, and that this vocabulary vanishes the moment marriage takes her to Calcutta, where the language of value, inspection and exchange takes over.

Files: subha.txt (corpus) · partial_reading.txt · initial_impression.md · prompt.txt · Subha_Themes.py (extracted themes) · visualise.py · interpretation.md

Visible assignment work

Method (seven steps). (1) Partial reading: the story’s 118 sentences were split into three equal parts and only 20% of each was read (first, random, last). (2) Initial impression and research problem, with frameworks from Spivak, ecofeminism and disability studies. (3) The full text was uploaded and cleaned. (4) A detailed prompt was written. (5) A local LLM (qwen2.5:7b via Ollama) extracted expressions, which were checked against the text and then annotated by the author. (6) Python visualisations. (7) Interpretation.

Statement tested: as the story moves from Chandipur to Calcutta, the vocabulary of nature and non-human kinship gives way to the vocabulary of social and patriarchal structure.

Findings. Voice and nature peak together where “there were only dumb Nature and a dumb girl”: the cows “understood her gentle murmuring better than all speech”. Nature returns once as farewell to her “dear river” and to “earth, her strong silent mother”, then drops to zero in the Calcutta section. There social structure peaks: she is inspected, found to be a “useful possession” and “delivered over… into another’s hands”. Her voice survives only as tears and gaze that “no one understood”.

Interpretation. Read with Spivak’s “Can the Subaltern Speak?”, Subha’s problem is not that she cannot speak but that no structure exists to hear her: “because she did not speak, therefore she did not feel” is epistemic violence. Gayle Rubin’s “traffic in women” explains the commercial vocabulary of the marriage, and ecofeminism (Plumwood) shows the double edge of her bond with nature: it sustains her, yet she ends as “a stricken doe” before “the hunter’s face”. The full argument, limitations and works cited are in interpretation.md.

Assignment 04

Creating a SKILL for an LLM: Indian Case-Brief Writer

Abstract. A SKILL file is a set of Markdown instructions that turns a general-purpose LLM into a reliable assistant for one specific task. This skill teaches an LLM to write structured case briefs of Indian Supreme Court and High Court judgments, especially fundamental-rights cases such as the privacy line from Kharak Singh to Govind and R. Rajagopal. It requires the model to work only from the judgment text supplied, to separate the ratio decidendi from obiter dicta and majority from dissent, and to quote and cite nothing that is not in that text.

File: SKILL.md

Visible assignment work

Name of the work: writing citation-faithful case briefs of Indian constitutional-law judgments.

What the skill makes the LLM do (eleven-step workflow):

  1. Identify the case, court, bench and citation exactly as given, never inventing them.
  2. Separate the majority, concurring and dissenting opinions.
  3. Extract the material facts, the issues as questions of law, and both sides’ arguments.
  4. State the holding and reasoning, naming the constitutional provisions and tests used (e.g. Art. 21, reasonable restriction, proportionality).
  5. Isolate the ratio decidendi, distinguish obiter dicta, and place the case among earlier and later precedents.
  6. Check that every quotation and paragraph number really appears in the judgment before answering.

Output: a fixed Markdown brief (Facts · Issues · Arguments · Holding · Reasoning · Ratio · Obiter · Dissent · Place in doctrine · Key quotations), with a comparison table when several cases are briefed together.

Design choice. Most errors in AI legal summaries are invented citations and dissents presented as law. The skill therefore puts accuracy rules ahead of style and keeps the court’s reasoning separate from any commentary.

02

Portfolio reflection

The same lesson ran through all four assignments: a language model is a fast reader but not a reliable one, and digital humanities work is mostly the design of checks around it. The Hindi stop-word list had to decide where grammar ends and meaning begins (negation words carry sentiment). The RAG pipeline produced 165 “plants and animals”, of which only 101 were real; the rest showed how closely Kalidasa ties nature to ornament and ritual. In “Subha”, the model’s first run collapsed, and the extracted categories were only meaningful after I annotated them against the text and my own research question. The SKILL for legal briefs turned these lessons into instructions: work only from the source, separate reporting from interpretation, and never invent a citation. Computation helped me see patterns, such as nature’s vocabulary vanishing when Subha reaches Calcutta, but interpretation still needed theory and close reading.

Optional: methods note

Tools: Python 3, Ollama running qwen2.5:7b and nomic-embed-text locally (no text was sent to a cloud service), NumPy and Matplotlib, GitHub Pages. All literary texts are public domain (Project Gutenberg). Every LLM output was checked automatically against the source text, and every manual correction is recorded in the code (validate_results.py, Subha_Themes.py). Failed runs are kept for transparency.
