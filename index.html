Khushi Jha’s Digital Humanities Portfolio

Portfolio introduction. These six assignments ask what computational methods can and cannot do with Indian languages and literature. They move from preparing Hindi, Bengali, Gujarati and Tamil text for NLP, to retrieving the plants and animals of five Indian books with a local RAG pipeline, to a synthetic reading of Tagore’s “Subha” that tests a literary argument with an LLM and visualisation, and to writing a SKILL that makes an LLM brief Indian constitutional judgments faithfully. Two additional assignments extend this work: one tests the stop-word lists on real text, and one compares the natural worlds of the five books. Each exhibition below links to its files on GitHub.

Assignment 01

NLP: Indic Stop Words — Hindi, Bengali, Gujarati and Tamil

Abstract. Stop words are high-frequency function words that carry little content on their own and are usually removed before keyword extraction, topic modelling or frequency analysis. I compiled stop-word lists for four Indic languages in four scripts: Hindi (Devanagari), Bengali (Bengali script) and Gujarati (Gujarati script), which are Indo-Aryan, and Tamil (Tamil script), which is Dravidian. Each list is grouped by grammatical category (conjunctions, pronouns, possessives, postpositions, question words, quantifiers, auxiliary verbs and particles), and each file has a small function that removes the stop words from a sentence.

Files: hindi_stopwords.py (235 words) · bengali_stopwords.py (210) · gujarati_stopwords.py (196) · tamil_stopwords.py (179)

Visible assignment work

A sample from each list:

  • Hindi: और · लेकिन · मैं · वह · का · में · से · क्या · है · नहीं
  • Bengali: এবং · কিন্তু · আমি · সে · থেকে · জন্য · কী · আছে · ছিল · না
  • Gujarati: અને · પરંતુ · હું · તે · માં · થી · શું · છે · હતું · નથી
  • Tamil: மற்றும் · ஆனால் · நான் · அவர் · இந்த · பற்றி · என்ன · உள்ளது · இல்லை

Example. The same sentence, “Today I went to the market with my friend and bought books there”, before and after removing stop words:

  • Hindi: मैं आज अपने दोस्त के साथ बाज़ार गई और वहाँ से किताबें खरीदीं → दोस्त बाज़ार किताबें खरीदीं
  • Bengali: আমি আজ বন্ধুর সঙ্গে বাজারে গিয়েছিলাম এবং সেখানে কিছু বই কিনলাম → বন্ধুর বাজারে গিয়েছিলাম বই কিনলাম
  • Gujarati: હું આજે મારા મિત્ર સાથે બજારમાં ગઈ અને ત્યાંથી પુસ્તકો ખરીદ્યાં → મિત્ર બજારમાં ત્યાંથી પુસ્તકો ખરીદ્યાં
  • Tamil: நான் இன்று என் நண்பருடன் சந்தைக்குச் சென்றேன் மற்றும் அங்கு சில புத்தகங்கள் வாங்கினேன் → நண்பருடன் சந்தைக்குச் சென்றேன் புத்தகங்கள் வாங்கினேன்

What comparing the languages showed. A stop-word list works best where grammar words stand apart from content words. In Hindi, postpositions are written as separate words (दोस्त के साथ), so they are removed cleanly. In Bengali and Gujarati, case endings are often joined to the noun (বাজারে, બજારમાં, “in the market”). In Tamil, which is agglutinative, “with my friend” is one word (நண்பருடன்). For these languages a list catches only the free-standing words, and fuller cleaning needs a stemmer or morphological analyser.

Limitations. Spelling varies (गए / गये, কখনো / কখনও), so common variants are listed together. The Bengali, Gujarati and Tamil files normalise text to Unicode NFC, because the same letter can be typed in two ways. Negation words such as नहीं, না, નથી and இல்லை change meaning, so they should be kept for tasks like sentiment analysis.

Assignment 02

Extraction of Indian Cultural Elements Using RAG: Flora and Fauna in Five Indian Books

Abstract. I built a repository of five Indian literary texts that differ in language of origin, period and genre: Kalidasa’s Sanskrit drama and poetry, the Pali Jataka fables, Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay’s Bengali novel The Poison Tree, Sarojini Naidu’s The Golden Threshold and Tagore’s The Gardener. A Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline that runs entirely on a local machine retrieved the passages most relevant to plants and animals in each book, and a local LLM extracted their names. Every name was checked against its source passage and validated, giving 65 plants and 68 animals.

Result file: flora_fauna.py

Corpus: Kalidasa · Jataka Tales · The Poison Tree · The Golden Threshold · The Gardener

Pipeline: preprocess.py · rag_pipeline.py · validate_results.py · make_flora_fauna.py · validated.json (each name with its evidence sentence)

Visible assignment work

Pipeline. (1) Pre-process: remove the Project Gutenberg licence and keep only the literary text, cutting translators’ introductions, prefaces, indexes and footnotes. (2) Chunk into 844 overlapping passages of about 250 words. (3) Embed each passage with nomic-embed-text. (4) Retrieve the 10 most similar passages from each book for each of 12 queries (trees, flowers, creepers, crops, mammals, birds, reptiles, fish, insects…), 238 passages in all, so that long books cannot crowd out short ones. (5) Generate: qwen2.5:7b lists the plant and animal names in each passage as JSON. (6) Verify that each name occurs in its passage. (7) Validate it (the model, plus 88 recorded author corrections) and merge variants. (8) Save as flora_fauna.py.

Flora (65): amaranth, ashoka, babul (acacia), bakul, bamboo, banyan, barley, bel (wood-apple), betel-nut, brinjal, casuarina, champa, coconut, coral tree, corn, cucumber, deodar, durva grass, fig, gourd, hemp, henna, hyacinth, jackfruit, jasmine, jujube, kadam, ketak (screw-pine), kusum, lichi, lily, linseed, lodh, lotus, madder, mango, mushroom, mustard, neem, nipa, oleander, orange, palm, panic grass, peach, plantain, plum, pomegranate, poppy, pumpkin, reed, rice, rose, rose-apple, sacred grass (kusha), saffron, sandalwood, silk cotton tree, siris, spring creeper, tamal, trumpet-flower, tulip, tulsi, water lily.

Fauna (68): antelope, ape, bat, bear, bee, bison, boar, buffalo, bull, butterfly, carp, cat, centipede, cobra, cockroach, cow, crane, crow, cuckoo, dahuk (waterhen), deer, dhadikula, dog, dove, duck, elephant, firefly, fly, frog, gazelle, goat, goose, gull, halcyon, hare, heron, horse, jackal, kite, koel, lion, lizard, monkey, mouse, musk deer, musk-rat, nightingale, oriole, owl, ox, paddy bird (pond heron), panther, papiya (hawk-cuckoo), parrot, partridge, peacock, pig, pigeon, plover, quail, rat, sheep, snake, sparrow, spider, squirrel, starling, swan.

What the validation step showed. Of 392 names proposed by the LLM, 33 did not occur in the passage at all, and 134 were not living things: ornaments (“lotus-bracelet”), food (“sausages”, “molasses”, which the model filed as an insect), objects (a bonti, the Bengali fish-cutter, filed as a fish), places, rivers and characters. Only 225 were real plants or animals. Some errors are themselves findings. In Kalidasa, nature is bound up with ornament and ritual (bark dresses, the “vine-bride”). In Bankim, the heroine Kunda is named after a jasmine, and the text plays on it: “what if you should discover the flower, Kunda, under the trees?”

Limitations. Names come from English translations, so some are the translators’ choices (“nightingale” for an Indian bird). Retrieval finds the most relevant passages, not every mention, which is why Assignment 06 counts mentions in the full texts.

Assignment 03

Synthetic Reading: Voice, Silence and Nature in Tagore’s “Subha”

Abstract. “Subha” is the story of a mute village girl whose family treats her as a “silent weight” and finally marries her off without revealing her muteness. After a partial reading of 20% of the story, I asked how the text makes speech the condition for being recognised as a feeling person, and where Subha’s own language goes when no human hears it. A local LLM extracted expressions along four dimensions (voice and silence, nature and non-human kinship, social and patriarchal structure, affect) across three stages of the story. The visualisation shows that Subha’s language lives with the river and the cows, and that this vocabulary vanishes the moment marriage takes her to Calcutta, where the language of value, inspection and exchange takes over.

Files: subha.txt (corpus) · partial_reading.txt · initial_impression.md · prompt.txt · subha_themes.py (extracted themes) · visualise.py · interpretation.md

Visible assignment work

Method (seven steps). (1) Partial reading: the story’s 118 sentences were split into three equal parts and only 20% of each was read (first, random, last). (2) Initial impression and research problem, with frameworks from Spivak, ecofeminism and disability studies. (3) The full text was uploaded and cleaned. (4) A detailed prompt was written. (5) A local LLM (qwen2.5:7b via Ollama) extracted expressions, which were checked against the text and then annotated by the author. (6) Python visualisations. (7) Interpretation.

Statement tested: as the story moves from Chandipur to Calcutta, the vocabulary of nature and non-human kinship gives way to the vocabulary of social and patriarchal structure.

Findings. Voice and nature peak together where “there were only dumb Nature and a dumb girl”: the cows “understood her gentle murmuring better than all speech”. Nature returns once as farewell to her “dear river” and to “earth, her strong silent mother”, then drops to zero in the Calcutta section. There social structure peaks: she is inspected, found to be a “useful possession” and “delivered over… into another’s hands”. Her voice survives only as tears and gaze that “no one understood”.

Interpretation. Read with Spivak’s “Can the Subaltern Speak?”, Subha’s problem is not that she cannot speak but that no structure exists to hear her: “because she did not speak, therefore she did not feel” is epistemic violence. Gayle Rubin’s “traffic in women” explains the commercial vocabulary of the marriage, and ecofeminism (Plumwood) shows the double edge of her bond with nature: it sustains her, yet she ends as “a stricken doe” before “the hunter’s face”. The full argument, limitations and works cited are in interpretation.md.

Assignment 04

Creating a SKILL for an LLM: Indian Case-Brief Writer

Abstract. A SKILL file is a set of Markdown instructions that turns a general-purpose LLM into a reliable assistant for one specific task. This skill teaches an LLM to write structured case briefs of Indian Supreme Court and High Court judgments, especially fundamental-rights cases such as the privacy line from Kharak Singh to Govind and R. Rajagopal. It requires the model to work only from the judgment text supplied, to separate the ratio decidendi from obiter dicta and majority from dissent, and to quote and cite nothing that is not in that text.

File: SKILL.md

Visible assignment work

Name of the work: writing citation-faithful case briefs of Indian constitutional-law judgments.

What the skill makes the LLM do (eleven-step workflow):

  1. Identify the case, court, bench and citation exactly as given, never inventing them.
  2. Separate the majority, concurring and dissenting opinions.
  3. Extract the material facts, the issues as questions of law, and both sides’ arguments.
  4. State the holding and reasoning, naming the constitutional provisions and tests used (e.g. Art. 21, reasonable restriction, proportionality).
  5. Isolate the ratio decidendi, distinguish obiter dicta, and place the case among earlier and later precedents.
  6. Check that every quotation and paragraph number really appears in the judgment before answering.

Output: a fixed Markdown brief (Facts · Issues · Arguments · Holding · Reasoning · Ratio · Obiter · Dissent · Place in doctrine · Key quotations), with a comparison table when several cases are briefed together.

Design choice. Most errors in AI legal summaries are invented citations and dissents presented as law. The skill therefore puts accuracy rules ahead of style and keeps the court’s reasoning separate from any commentary.

Assignment 05

Testing the Stop-Word Lists on Real Text: Why Tamil Resists a List

Abstract. An additional assignment extending Assignment 01. I tested the four stop-word lists from Assignment 01 by using them on comparable texts: the same four Wikipedia articles (India, Rabindranath Tagore, Monsoon, Ganges) in Hindi, Bengali, Gujarati and Tamil, about 48,000 tokens in all. The Hindi list removes 41.2% of all tokens, the Gujarati list 28.6%, the Bengali list 19.3% and the Tamil list only 14.5%. The difference comes from grammar: Hindi writes its postpositions as separate words, while Tamil and Bengali join case endings to the noun (இந்தியாவின், ভারতের, “of India”), where a whitespace-based list cannot reach them.

Files: evaluate_stopwords.py · findings.md · top_words.md (frequency tables) · coverage.json · texts/

Visible assignment work

Findings. (1) Lists of similar size remove very different shares of text, so the gap is caused by grammar, not by list length. (2) Postpositions do 46% of the removal work in Hindi but only 9% in Tamil, where “of India” and “in India” are single words. (3) The most frequent words the lists did not remove point to additions, such as Hindi जाता, Bengali সালে, Gujarati તરીકે and Tamil இல். (4) Between 59% and 71% of each list occurs in the texts. Colloquial forms such as तू and তুই never appear in encyclopaedia prose, so the right list depends on the genre.

Conclusion. Stop-word removal is not language-neutral. It suits languages that write grammar as separate words, and for agglutinative languages such as Tamil it needs a morphological analyser rather than a longer list.

Assignment 06

Comparing the Natural Worlds of Five Indian Books

Abstract. An additional assignment extending Assignment 02. The RAG pipeline found which plants and animals the five books name. This assignment counts how often each name is mentioned in the full text of every book, and compares the books. Each genre turns out to have its own nature: the Jataka fables are 77% animals, Sarojini Naidu’s poems are 65% plants (38% flowers alone) and the densest at 17 mentions per 1,000 words, and Bankim’s novel is full of household creatures (owls, sparrows, lizards, rats, cats). Only six names occur in all five books: deer, lotus, mango, peacock, rice and snake.

Files: compare_books.py · findings.md · summary.md · mentions.json

Visible assignment work

Counting needs reading. Some names are also English words or characters. A first count gave Naidu’s poems six bears, from “we bear her along”, and found the flower “kunda” 400 times in The Poison Tree, where Kunda is the heroine. Such names are counted only in plant or animal contexts. Every rule is in the code.

02

Portfolio reflection

The same lesson ran through all six assignments: a language model is a fast reader but not a reliable one, and digital humanities work is mostly the design of checks around it. The stop-word lists had to decide where grammar ends and meaning begins, and testing them showed that the method itself favours Hindi over Tamil. The RAG pipeline proposed 392 “plants and animals”, of which only 225 were real. Counting them across five books showed how much the meaning of a word depends on reading it in context: a verb, a pun, a heroine. In “Subha”, the model’s first run collapsed, and the extracted categories were only meaningful after I annotated them against the text and my own research question. The SKILL for legal briefs turned these lessons into instructions: work only from the source, separate reporting from interpretation, and never invent a citation. Computation helped me see patterns, such as nature’s vocabulary vanishing when Subha reaches Calcutta, but interpretation still needed theory and close reading.

Optional: methods note

Tools: Python 3, Ollama running qwen2.5:7b and nomic-embed-text locally (no text was sent to a cloud service), NumPy, Matplotlib and NetworkX, GitHub Pages. All literary texts are public domain (Project Gutenberg). Every LLM output was checked automatically against the source text, and every manual correction is recorded in the code (validate_results.py, subha_themes.py, compare_books.py). Failed runs are kept for transparency.
